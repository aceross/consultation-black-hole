# "We are analysing your feedback"

*How long the government takes to answer its own consultations, and how often it never does*

---

When a government consultation closes on GOV.UK, the button to respond disappears. In its place sits a short banner: "We are analysing your feedback. Visit this page again soon to download the outcome to this public feedback."

The government's own [Consultation Principles](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/consultation-principles-guidance/consultation-principles-2018), last revised in 2018, set out what is supposed to follow. The principles do not carry legal force, yet they are plain about what participants can expect. Departments should publish their response on the same page as the consultation, and they should do so within 12 weeks of closing, or explain why that is not possible.

I wanted to know how often that happens.

Unanswered consultations are not a new complaint. Journalists and MPs have counted them [before](https://psnews.com.au/united-kingdom-silence-over-consultations-condemned/90684/), usually by picking out striking individual cases, and watchdogs such as the Institute for Government and the Hansard Society have criticised short consultation windows and slow follow-through. Neither publishes an ongoing timeliness series across Whitehall. GOV.UK, however, exposes enough structured data to measure the whole record. Every consultation page carries its closing date and a dated change history showing when updates were added.

So I collected the lot and timed the wait.

## Measuring the wait

I downloaded every consultation on GOV.UK that closed between January 2019 and 23 November 2026. That gave 3,343 consultation pages across 104 organisations, plus one retired redirect. For each page, I read its change history and took the first entry after the closing date that added a substantive response, outcome, summary of responses or decision, filtering out holding notes that merely announce a delay or count how many replies arrived.

The main figures below cover ministerial departments plus HMRC. That gives 2,185 consultations across 19 department groupings, 17 of which have closed consultations since the July 2024 election. Successor departments are grouped with their predecessors, including the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade (BIST) created in July 2026 alongside DBT. I left aside regulators and arm's-length bodies because they follow different practices, and many of their "consultations" are routine operational notices, such as monthly fishing catch limits, that never receive a formal response.

Because recent consultations have not had long to receive a reply, I used Kaplan-Meier survival estimates to track response rates by week. This lets consultations that closed a few months ago count for the weeks they have waited without treating them as permanent failures.

## Only one in five gets an answer on time

Since the July 2024 election, roughly one in five departmental consultations has received a response on its GOV.UK page within 12 weeks, down from around one in four before polling day.

- **Closed 2019 to 2021** (773 consultations): **27%** answered within 12 weeks, **52%** within 26 weeks, **72%** within a year and **83%** within two years
- **Closed 2022 to the July 2024 election** (779 consultations): **26%** within 12 weeks, **52%** within 26 weeks, **71%** within a year and **82%** within two years
- **Closed since the July 2024 election** (633 consultations): **20%** within 12 weeks, **52%** within 26 weeks, **79%** within a year and **87%\*** within two years

\* Only five consultations in the post-election group have been closed for two full years (remaining at risk at week 104), so the two-year figure is the least certain.

![Kaplan-Meier step curves showing the share of ministerial department and HMRC consultations answered on GOV.UK by weeks since closing: 20% by week 12 and 52% by week 26 since the July 2024 election (633 consultations), compared with 26% and 52% in 2022–2024 (779) and 27% and 52% in 2019–2021 (773).](figures/fig1_response_curves.png)

Across all three periods, the midpoint sits at almost exactly six months, with 52% answered by week 26. After two years, roughly one in six still has nothing on the page.

The post-election group catches up later in the year, reaching 79% at 52 weeks compared with 71% to 72% for the two earlier periods. Where it has slipped is at the 12-week mark, the only deadline the principles actually specify.

## A wide gap between departments

To check individual departments without survival adjustments, I looked at the 558 departmental consultations that closed from 5 July 2024 and more than 12 weeks before 24 November 2026. Of those, 112 (20%) received a substantive response on the page within 12 weeks, and 155 (28%) still had none.

Performance varies sharply across the 14 departments with at least 10 eligible consultations, which together account for 546 of the 558 total. DCMS leads at 40% answered within 12 weeks (4 of 10), followed by the Department for Education on 33% (10 of 30) and DSIT (3 of 10, with none left unanswered) and HMRC (13 of 44) on 30%. At the lower end sit Defra on 11% (7 of 61), HM Treasury on 10% (3 of 29) and the Ministry of Justice on 0% (0 of 15). Three departments had fewer than 10 eligible consultations and are omitted from the chart: the Cabinet Office (0 of 4 within 12 weeks, 1 unanswered), the Ministry of Defence (0 of 5, 2 unanswered) and the Northern Ireland Office (1 of 3, 2 unanswered on the page).

![Horizontal bar scorecard of the 14 departments with 10+ eligible post-election consultations: DCMS leads at 40% within 12 weeks, HMRC and DSIT are at 30%, Defra sits at 11% (with 43% still unanswered), HM Treasury is 10% (median 158 days), and Ministry of Justice is 0% (60% unanswered).](figures/fig2_departments.png)

Looking only at the 12-week bar hides two quite different patterns at the bottom of the table. HM Treasury almost never answers within 12 weeks (10%), yet it usually answers in the end. Only four of its 29 eligible consultations remain unanswered, with a median wait of 158 days among those that received a reply. The Ministry of Justice has missed the 12-week mark on all 15 of its eligible consultations, takes a median of 195.5 days when it does respond, and still has no response on GOV.UK for 9 of them (60%).

Defra's figures require a closer look because a naive keyword search on change histories puts it near the top of the table, with nearly half seemingly answered within 12 weeks. Reading the notes explains why. On 30 of its 61 eligible consultations, 22 of them between 62 and 84 days after closing, Defra posted a holding notice saying responses were still being analysed or publication was delayed.

Sometimes that explanation makes obvious sense. On the 12-week deadline for its consultation on [prohibiting trail hunting in England and Wales](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/proposals-to-prohibit-trail-hunting-in-england-and-wales) (closed 18 June 2026, updated 10 September 2026), Defra noted that it had received 184,000 responses and was delaying publication to consider them carefully. On other occasions the pattern looks more routine. Twelve weeks after closing its consultation on a [proposed fisheries management plan for wrasses](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/wrasses-complex-proposed-fisheries-management-plan-consultation) in May 2026 (updated 28 July 2026), Defra posted a note stating "We received 11 responses, which we’re still analysing" and pushed the expected reply to the end of 2026. Thirteen of Defra's 30 holding-note consultations still have no substantive response at all. Once holding notices are separated from answers, Defra drops to 11% within 12 weeks (7 of 61), with 43% (26) still unanswered.

A change of government explains very little of the post-election backlog. Only 24 of the 558 post-election consultations opened before polling day on 5 July 2024, and just three remain unanswered:

- DBT's (now assigned to BIST) [*Smarter regulation: employment law reform*](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/smarter-regulation-employment-law-reform) (closed 11 July 2024)
- DHSC's [*Proposal for a 'tie-in' to NHS dentistry for graduate dentists*](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/proposal-for-a-tie-in-to-nhs-dentistry-for-graduate-dentists) (closed 18 July 2024)
- DWP's [*Modernising support for independent living: the health and disability green paper*](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/modernising-support-for-independent-living-the-health-and-disability-green-paper) (closed 22 July 2024)

The change of Prime Minister in July 2026 has also not produced a comparable overhang. Of 28 closed departmental consultations tagged to the Burnham government, only one had passed the 12-week mark by 24 November 2026 (none overdue), whereas 152 overdue departmental consultations belong to the 2024–2026 Starmer government.

## When the answer is somewhere else

This measures what appears on the consultation page, not whether policy decisions were communicated elsewhere. A department may drop a proposal quietly, absorb it into a Budget document or answer points in the explanatory memorandum to a statutory instrument without updating the original consultation page. That is a real limit of page-level measurement, though an off-page reply still departs from the principles, which instruct departments to publish the response on the same page so participants can find it.

To see how often answers sit elsewhere on GOV.UK, I checked 30 post-election pages marked as having no response yet (all four from HM Treasury, all 14 from HMRC and the 12 oldest across other departments) plus three outcome pages whose change history recorded nothing after closing. A few had clear off-page or unlogged outcomes. The Northern Ireland Office's [non-jury trials consultation](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/northern-ireland-office-launches-consultation-on-the-use-of-non-jury-trials-in-northern-ireland) received a [separate news announcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/consultation-response-on-non-jury-trials-in-northern-ireland-may-2025) within 12 weeks without updating the consultation page, and HM Treasury's Alternative Investment Fund Managers consultation received a later [draft statutory instrument and policy note](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/alternative-investment-fund-managers-regulation-draft-si-and-policy-note). Two outcome pages (HM Treasury on Consumer Credit Act reform and DfT on a Chiltern Railways accessibility exemption) contained outcome text without a post-closing change-history entry. For most of the rest, no published outcome was located on GOV.UK.

Three dataset details also bear noting:

- **Dates come from GOV.UK's change history.** Where a page was updated in several stages, I took the first substantive entry after filtering out holding notes. A spot-check of eight pages in the initial October extract found the dates matched the published responses, though the method can still catch an interim legislative step ahead of a full policy reply.
- **Some pages were never switched over.** Across 2019–2026, 25 pages still labelled as "closed" (9 in ministerial departments and HMRC) carried post-closing change-history notes recording a response. I counted those as answered.
- **Holding notes are tracked separately.** Across 2019–2026, 84 pages carry post-closing holding or delay notes, including 33 in the 558 post-election departmental group (30 of them from Defra).

## Keeping the other side of the bargain

A public consultation asks people outside government to spend time reading technical papers, gathering evidence and writing considered replies. Most respondents are not well-staffed trade associations. They are small charities, local councils, clinicians, academics and ordinary citizens working unpaid.

When the 12-week window passes and the page continues to promise "We are analysing your feedback. Visit this page again soon", a participant cannot tell whether officials are still reading submissions, whether ministers quietly shelved the proposal, or whether the answer was tucked into an annex to another document.

Fixing this would cost very little, because GOV.UK already holds the metadata needed to make the system legible. Every closed consultation banner could display the date the 12-week target falls due and, once that passes, how long the response has been waiting. Departments that drop a proposal or answer it in another publication could be required to post a two-sentence closing note with a link on the original page. And a department-by-department timeliness scorecard like the one above could be published automatically from GOV.UK's own APIs. Building it took an afternoon.

### A note on the data

**Sources.** Consultation metadata and full page histories come from the GOV.UK search API (all items of type `open_consultation`, `closed_consultation` and `consultation_outcome`) and content API for every consultation closing from 1 January 2019, refreshed on 24 November 2026. Three pages with pre-1990 typo `opening_date` years (`0025`, `0202`, `0020`) fall back to `first_public_at`.

**Response dates.** The response date is the first change-history entry after the closing date whose note mentions a response, outcome, feedback, summary, government reply, decision or conclusion, excluding pure holding or delay explanations. For `consultation_outcome` pages with no matching keyword note, it is the first non-holding entry after closing.

**Departments.** "Departments" covers ministerial departments under their current or predecessor titles plus HMRC. This groups BIST alongside DBT and DIT (`BIST/DBT`, as GOV.UK reassigned DBT's Make Work Pay and trade consultations to BIST after July 2026), BEIS with DESNZ, DLUHC and the 2018–2021 MHCLG with MHCLG, both earlier DCMS titles with DCMS, plus AGO and UKEF.

**Estimates.** Cohort shares are Kaplan-Meier estimates with unanswered consultations censored at 24 November 2026.

The extracted dataset, analysis scripts and figure scripts are available in the [companion GitHub repository](https://github.com/aceross/consultation-black-hole).
