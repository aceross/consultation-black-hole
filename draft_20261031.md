# "We are analysing your feedback"

*Whitehall aims to answer public consultations within 12 weeks. Four in five miss that target.*

---

When a government consultation closes on GOV.UK, the button inviting you to respond disappears. In its place sits a familiar message: "We are analysing your feedback. Visit this page again soon to download the outcome to this public feedback."

How soon is soon?

The government's own [Consultation Principles](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/consultation-principles-guidance/consultation-principles-2018), last revised in 2018, give departments 12 weeks to publish a response or explain why they need longer. Although the principles do not carry legal force, they are explicit that the response should appear on the same page as the original consultation, where the people who contributed can find it.

Since the change of government in July 2024, only one in five departmental consultations has received a recorded response on that page within 12 weeks. Three in four (76%) received neither a response nor a delay explanation within that window. Half take around six months, and some remain unanswered years after they closed.

I wanted to find out how widespread this problem was. Slow and unanswered consultations have attracted [criticism before](https://psnews.com.au/united-kingdom-silence-over-consultations-condemned/90684/), and watchdogs such as the Institute for Government and the Hansard Society have criticised short consultation windows and slow follow-through, though neither publishes an ongoing timeliness series across Whitehall. GOV.UK gives us the means to measure the whole record. Its consultation pages contain closing dates and dated histories of changes made to their contents.

So I collected the lot and timed the wait.

## How long does the government take to respond?

I downloaded the records of 3,343 consultations from 104 public organisations on GOV.UK, covering consultations that closed between January 2019 and 23 November 2026 (plus one retired redirect). For each consultation, I examined its update history to establish when the government first published a substantive response, outcome, summary of responses or decision. Announcements that officials were still analysing submissions or needed more time were recorded separately.

For the main analysis, I concentrated on ministerial departments and HMRC. This left 2,185 consultations across 19 department groupings, 17 of which have closed consultations since July 2024. Regulators and arm's-length bodies were excluded because they often consult for different purposes, including routine operational notices such as monthly fishing catch limits that do not generate a formal response.

The first thing that struck me was how similar the three periods are. Whether a consultation closed in 2019, 2023 or after the July 2024 election, around 52% had received a recorded response by 26 weeks. The government appears to have settled into responding in roughly twice the time its own guidance envisages.

At the 12-week mark, the only deadline the principles specify, the share answered on time has fallen:

- **2019 to 2021** (773 consultations): **27%** had received a recorded response within 12 weeks
- **2022 to July 2024** (779 consultations): **26%** within 12 weeks
- **Since July 2024** (633 consultations): **20%** within 12 weeks

There has been some improvement further down the line. Accounting for how long recent consultations have been open, an estimated 79% of the post-July 2024 cohort received a recorded response within a year, compared with 71% to 72% in the earlier periods. Getting a timely answer, however, has become less common. In both earlier periods with full two-year follow-up, around one in six consultations (83% answered in 2019–2021 and 82% in 2022–2024) still had no recorded response on its original page after two years.

## It depends which department you ask

Of the 558 departmental consultations that closed from 5 July 2024 and had been closed for at least 12 weeks by 24 November 2026, just 112 (20%) received a response within the target period. Another 291 (52%) were answered later, and 155 (28%) still had no recorded response on GOV.UK.

Some departments perform noticeably better than others, though even those at the top answer fewer than half within 12 weeks. Across the 14 departments with at least 10 eligible consultations (546 of the 558 total), the outcomes split into distinct patterns.

![Horizontal stacked bar chart showing three outcomes for the 14 departments with 10+ eligible post-election consultations: DCMS leads on 40% answered within 12 weeks and DfE reaches 33%, DSIT has 0% unanswered (30% within 12w, 70% later), HM Treasury shows 10% within 12w and 76% later against 14% unanswered, Defra sits at 11% within 12w with 43% unanswered, and Ministry of Justice has 0% within 12w with 60% unanswered.](figures/fig2_departments.png)

DCMS comes out on top, having answered four of its ten eligible consultations within 12 weeks (40%). The Department for Education answered ten of its thirty (33%), while DSIT (three of ten on time, with all ten answered) and HMRC (13 of 44) sit at 30%.

At the other end of the chart, two departments stand out for quite different reasons.

HM Treasury rarely responds within 12 weeks. Only three of its 29 consultations met that target (10%). Yet 22 more received an answer later (76%), leaving just four without a recorded response (14%).

The Ministry of Justice has a different problem. None of its 15 eligible consultations received a response within 12 weeks (0%), six were answered later (40%), and nine still had no recorded answer on GOV.UK (60%).

Among the consultations that have received a response, the median wait was 158 days (around five months) at the Treasury and 195.5 days (around six and a half months, excluding the nine still unanswered) at the Ministry of Justice.

Waiting a few extra months for an answer is one thing. Waiting without knowing whether an answer will ever come is quite another.

Nor can much of the current backlog be attributed to consultations inherited from the previous government. Of the 558 consultations examined here, only 24 were opened before 5 July 2024, when the government changed after the 4 July general election. Just three of those remained unanswered: DBT's (now assigned to BIST) [employment law reform](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/smarter-regulation-employment-law-reform), DHSC's [proposal for a tie-in to NHS dentistry for graduate dentists](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/proposal-for-a-tie-in-to-nhs-dentistry-for-graduate-dentists), and DWP's [reforms to health and disability support](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/modernising-support-for-independent-living-the-health-and-disability-green-paper). Most of the outstanding consultations were launched under the new government.

## Still analysing

The Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) presented an instructive problem when I was putting the data together. An initial search of its consultation histories suggested it was one of Whitehall's better performers, with nearly half appearing to receive an update within 12 weeks.

Then I looked closely at what those updates actually said. On 30 of its 61 eligible consultations, Defra had published a holding notice explaining that responses were still being analysed or publication had been delayed. In 22 cases, these notices appeared between 62 and 84 days after closing without a substantive response.

Some of those delays are readily understandable. Defra received 184,000 submissions to its consultation on [prohibiting trail hunting in England and Wales](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/proposals-to-prohibit-trail-hunting-in-england-and-wales). On 10 September 2026, twelve weeks after it closed on 18 June, the department noted the volume of responses and explained that it needed more time to consider them carefully.

Other consultations received far fewer submissions. In July 2026, twelve weeks after closing its consultation on a [proposed fisheries management plan for wrasses](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/wrasses-complex-proposed-fisheries-management-plan-consultation), Defra updated the page: "We received 11 responses, which we’re still analysing." It expected to publish the outcome by the end of the year. Eleven submissions can still raise difficult technical questions, and response volume alone does not settle how long analysis should take. Even so, the contrast illustrates the range of consultations for which departments issue these notices.

Defra deserves credit for telling participants that their submissions are still being considered, since the Consultation Principles expressly allow departments to explain why a reply is taking longer than 12 weeks. In fact, Defra accounts for 22 of the 24 post-election departmental consultations where a delay explanation appeared within 12 weeks without a substantive reply. Across the remaining 497 consultations from the other 16 departments, only two carried a timely holding notice (one each from the Ministry of Defence and BIST/DBT).

Taken together, 422 of the 558 post-election consultations (76%) received neither a recorded response nor an explanation for delay within 12 weeks, slightly higher than the 72% recorded in both the 2019–2021 and 2022–July 2024 periods.

Once holding notices are separated from answers, Defra's substantive record looks very different. Only seven of its 61 consultations received an answer within 12 weeks (11%). Thirteen of the 30 holding-note consultations remain unanswered, leaving 26 (43%) with no recorded response overall. Measuring government performance through website updates tells us very little until we read what was updated.

## Perhaps the answer is somewhere else

An unanswered consultation page does not necessarily mean the government has done nothing. A proposal may have been abandoned quietly, absorbed into a Budget announcement or addressed in the explanatory notes to secondary legislation without anyone updating the consultation page.

To see how often answers sit elsewhere on GOV.UK, I checked 30 post-election pages marked as unanswered in the data (all four from HM Treasury, all 14 from HMRC and the 12 oldest across other departments), along with all three post-election departmental outcome pages whose update histories recorded nothing after closing.

I found a handful of off-page or unlogged replies. The Northern Ireland Office's [consultation on non-jury trials](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/northern-ireland-office-launches-consultation-on-the-use-of-non-jury-trials-in-northern-ireland) did receive a substantive response within 12 weeks, published as a [separate news announcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/consultation-response-on-non-jury-trials-in-northern-ireland-may-2025) rather than added to the original page. HM Treasury also published [draft regulations and a policy note](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/alternative-investment-fund-managers-regulation-draft-si-and-policy-note) following its consultation on alternative investment fund managers. Of the three outcome pages without a post-closing update entry, one was a Defra holding notice on packaging waste recycling notes, while two (HM Treasury on Consumer Credit Act reform and DfT on a Chiltern Railways accessibility exemption) contained substantive outcome text edited directly onto the page without logging a change note. Counting those two unlogged outcomes would bring the post-election total without a recorded response from 155 down to 153 (and trim HM Treasury from four to three, and DfT from 18 to 17). For most of the remaining pages I inspected, however, no published outcome was located on GOV.UK.

These checks cannot show how common unlogged or misplaced responses are across government, though they do expose a weakness in how consultations are published. The guidance specifically asks departments to publish responses on the original consultation page. Someone who contributed to a consultation should not have to search through press releases, policy papers and draft statutory instruments to discover what happened to it.

## Keeping the other side of the bargain

Responding to a government consultation takes real work. A small charity may gather evidence from the people it supports. A professional association or local council may canvass its members and technical staff. An individual citizen may spend an evening reading a dense policy document and writing out objections.

The government asks those people to contribute their time and expertise, often within a tight deadline. It seems reasonable that they should expect something clear in return: an account of what the government learned, what it intends to do and how their contributions informed that decision.

Departments cannot always deliver that within 12 weeks. Some proposals are legally intricate, some attract tens of thousands of submissions and ministerial priorities shift. There are legitimate reasons for an answer to take longer. What makes the current system frustrating is that participants are left unable to tell prolonged deliberation from an abandoned proposal or a decision published in another corner of Whitehall.

A few modest changes to GOV.UK would help. Closed consultation banners could display the date a response is due and how long it has been waiting once that passes. Departments needing longer could post a brief explanation and expected publication date, and where a proposal is dropped or answered elsewhere, a two-sentence closing note with a link on the original page could tell readers where to look. The Cabinet Office could also publish an automated departmental scorecard directly from GOV.UK's existing metadata.

Putting together the initial analysis for this article took an afternoon.

It should be at least as easy to find out what happened to a public consultation as it was to take part in it.

### A note on the data

**Sources.** Consultation metadata and page histories come from the GOV.UK search API (all items of type `open_consultation`, `closed_consultation` and `consultation_outcome`) and content API for consultations closing from 1 January 2019 to 23 November 2026, refreshed on 24 November 2026. Three records with pre-1990 typo `opening_date` years (`0025`, `0202`, `0020`) fall back to `first_public_at`.

**Identifying responses.** Response dates come from the first post-closing change-history entry mentioning a response, outcome, feedback, summary, government reply, decision or conclusion, excluding entries announcing delays or continued analysis. For `consultation_outcome` pages lacking a matching keyword note, the first non-holding entry after closing is used. A spot-check of eight pages in the initial October extract confirmed the recorded dates matched published responses, though the rule can catch an interim legislative step ahead of a final reply and misses outcomes edited onto a page without a change-history entry (such as the two HMT and DfT pages noted above, which are kept under the uniform update-history rule rather than assigning estimated dates). Across 2019–2026, 25 pages still labelled as closed consultations (9 in ministerial departments and HMRC) logged responses in their change history and are counted as answered. Holding notices are tracked separately, appearing on 84 pages overall and 33 in the 558 post-election departmental cohort (30 from Defra).

**Departmental coverage.** The main dataset covers 2,185 consultations across ministerial departments and HMRC (19 department groupings overall, 17 with closed consultations since 5 July 2024). Predecessor and successor bodies are grouped together, counting BIST (created in July 2026, to which GOV.UK reassigned DBT's Make Work Pay and trade consultations) alongside DBT and DIT (`BIST/DBT`), BEIS with DESNZ, DLUHC and 2018–2021 MHCLG with MHCLG, earlier DCMS titles with DCMS, plus AGO and UKEF. Three departments had fewer than 10 eligible post-election consultations and are omitted from Figure 2: the Cabinet Office (0 of 4 within 12 weeks, 1 unanswered), the Ministry of Defence (0 of 5, 2 unanswered) and the Northern Ireland Office (1 of 3, 2 unanswered on the page).

**Waiting times.** Cohort curves in Figure 1 are Kaplan-Meier estimates with consultations without a recorded response censored on 24 November 2026. Only five consultations in the post-July 2024 cohort remained under observation without a recorded response at week 104 (and fewer than 20 after week 82, shown dotted in Figure 1). In Figure 2, each department's share without a recorded response is partly shaped by how recently its eligible consultations closed.

The extracted dataset, analysis scripts and figure scripts are available in the [companion GitHub repository](https://github.com/aceross/consultation-black-hole).
